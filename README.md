Root water uptake (RWU) is a central process linking vegetation water use with subsurface moisture redistribution, yet the role of weathered bedrock as a water source in semi‑arid forest ecosystems remains poorly quantified. In this study, we develop a non‑parametric, observation‑driven diagnostic framework that integrates mass conservation with water‑demand allocation to infer layer‑wise evapotranspiration and RWU across aeolian‑sand-weathered‑bedrock profiles. Two Larch plantation plots underlain by sandstone and tuff bedrock in the semi‑arid Saihanba in North part of Yanshan Mountain were instrumented with high‑resolution measurements of volumetric water content, temperature, precipitation, soil evaporation, and sap flow. Both profiles exhibited strong seasonal stratification in moisture dynamics driven by freeze-thaw cycles, snowmelt infiltration, and growing‑season transpiration. Snowmelt replenished 81.5 mm of water in the sandstone profile and 12.4 mm in the tuff profile. Weathered bedrock contributed modest but functionally important RWU: about 5.18% of total transpiration in the sandstone profile, concentrated early in the growing season, and about 1.07% in the tuff profile during short periods of severe soil moisture deficit. During the post‑growing season, bedrock RWU accounted for 2.21% and 4.93% of total transpiration in the sandstone and tuff profiles, respectively. The diagnostic framework successfully reconstructed vertical water fluxes and revealed that weathered bedrock layers act as deep moisture buffers with slow drainage and stable water retention. These findings demonstrate that weathered bedrock provides ecologically meaningful water subsidies to shallow‑rooted plantations in semi‑arid regions, particularly during early‑season recharge and late‑season moisture decline. Incorporating bedrock moisture dynamics into evapotranspiration frameworks is essential for improving assessments of drought resilience and guiding water‑limited afforestation strategies in the northern China drylands.
